How the field works

Your walls, sources and drifters are saved in this browser as you go. Loading a map replaces them.

Drift runs from dissonant regions toward regions consonant with a drifter's chord, faster where that changes more steeply, never faster than the waves travel. A drifter moves at half the drift under it. The Drift setting scales how hard a slope pushes.

True pitch runs the clock at a hundredth of real frequency: A4 vibrates 4.40 times a second and every note at its own true rate. A cell is then about 7 cm and the map an 11 m room.

Absorption grows with the square of the frequency, so highs fade over distance far faster than lows: B5 loses about 14 times what C4 does. Low notes also bend around walls more, straight out of their longer waves.

Waveform is physical. A source sends out its fundamental and each harmonic of its waveform as separate waves: square has the odd harmonics at 1/n, saw every harmonic at 1/n, triangle the odd ones at 1/n², sine none. Shorter harmonics bend round walls less and fade faster, so a bright waveform goes dull behind walls and over distance. Harmonics too short for the grid are left out; a finer resolution keeps more.

Roughness is measured where you are, from the harmonics that actually arrive there. The table shows it at the sources; behind a wall the same two notes may agree better, because the wall has stripped the harmonics that grated. You hear the same thing: listening plays every harmonic at the strength it arrives.

The grid is hexagonal: every cell has six neighbours at the same distance, so waves travel at nearly the same speed in every direction. On a square grid short waves ran faster along the axes than across them, which drew a four-armed star around high notes.

The field is computed on your graphics card. It holds up to 64 waves: every harmonic of every note is its own wave.

Reflections splits every source's field by reflection order. Each order's walls absorb the waves that hit them and hand the same reflection a reflective wall makes to the next order, so direct sound casts true shadows and each order can be seen on its own. Added back together they are exactly the full field. The map boundary is not counted as a reflection.

With a reflective boundary and no absorption nothing ever leaves the map. Sound trapped behind walls rings on until absorption takes it.

Gain is how hard a source drives the field, in dB: it changes how far it carries, how much it weighs in dissonance and how it pulls on drifters, and you hear it too. The Sound settings and colours change what you hear and see only.
